# Walking Cadence: Steps per Minute That Count

**Walking cadence means steps per minute, counting both feet.** The public-health rule of thumb is about **100 steps per minute**. For many adults that rate sits near **3 METs**, the usual line for moderate intensity — what people mean when they say a walk should be brisk. It is a heuristic you can count on a sidewalk. It is not a lab printout for your height, your hills, or your shoes.

Tudor-Locke's narrative review in the *British Journal of Sports Medicine* put ~100 steps/min forward as a practical moderate-intensity cue. Rowe and colleagues, in *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise* (2011), tied that same ~3 MET line to step rate and showed the number moves with height. Use 100 as the default. Nudge it if you are much taller or much shorter than average. Then check that you can still talk.

## What walking cadence actually measures

One step is one footfall. Walking cadence adds every footfall in a minute: left, right, left, right. A rate of 100 is **100 footfalls**, about 50 per leg, not 100 per leg. Count both feet or the number will look brisk while you are still strolling.

Walking cadence is a rate. It is not distance, and it is not effort by itself. Two people can both hold 100 steps/min and cover different ground, because one takes a long stride and the other chops the step short. The useful question is whether the rate lines up with moderate effort for your body.

Miles per hour is the other common description. Walking cadence is easier to count without GPS. Typical adult speeds: [average walking pace](/blog/average-walking-pace).

## Why a walking cadence of about 100 steps per minute counts

**About 100 steps/min is the brisk-walking heuristic for moderate intensity, near 3 METs, for many adults.** Tudor-Locke's BJSM review treated it as a public-health shortcut: hold roughly 100 steps a minute and you are probably in the moderate band that activity guidelines care about. Rowe et al. (2011) measured the cadence that produced 3 METs and landed in the same neighborhood for people of average height.

Three METs means the work costs about three times resting energy. That is the floor of moderate, not a hard session.

Plain-language brisk, and how it differs from a commute stroll: [what is brisk walking](/blog/what-is-brisk-walking).

A second check is the **talk test**. Moderate effort means you can speak in sentences but not comfortably sing. If you can chat the whole way without breathing harder, the walk is probably easier than the 3 MET line, even if a round number on the watch looks fine. If you can only get out a few words, you have left that band. Heart-rate zones for the same effort idea: [heart rate zones calculator](/tools/heart-rate-zones-calculator). Easy aerobic walking that stays conversational for longer is the territory of [zone 2 walking](/blog/zone-2-walking).

## How height changes the 100-step target

**Height changes the walking cadence that hits 3 METs.** Rowe 2011 found roughly **90 steps/min** for a tall adult (~198 cm, about 6 ft 6 in) and about **113 steps/min** for a shorter adult (~152 cm, about 5 ft). Taller people need fewer steps per minute to hit the same intensity because their stride is longer. Each step covers more ground, so the same metabolic cost shows up at a lower step rate.

| Height (approx.) | Walking cadence near 3 METs (Rowe 2011) |
| --- | --- |
| ~198 cm (6 ft 6 in) | ~90 steps/min |
| Average adult | ~100 steps/min (heuristic) |
| ~152 cm (5 ft) | ~113 steps/min |

**Height-adjustment note.** Do not treat 100 as a law if you are far from average height. A 198 cm walker who chases 113 steps/min is working harder than the 3 MET line Rowe described. A 152 cm walker who stops at 90 is often still strolling. Start at 100. Nudge toward ~90 if you are very tall, or toward ~110–113 if you are shorter. Between those heights, slide toward 100 and let the talk test settle the last few steps per minute. Shoes, fatigue, and a headwind move effort too.

## Walking cadence bands used in research

Tudor-Locke walking cadence bands, often used in research, split everyday walking like this:

| Band | Steps per minute | Usual label |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Slow | 60–79 | Slow walking |
| Medium | 80–99 | Medium, purposeful, not yet brisk |
| Brisk | 100–119 | Brisk, near moderate intensity |
| Faster | 120+ | Faster ambulation |

Most useful health walking sits in **100–119**. Below 80 is a stroll. **80–99** is medium: you are going somewhere, and you are not yet at the 3 MET heuristic.

Two extra numbers get used next to 100. **About 120 steps/min** is sometimes treated as near **4 METs**. **Around 130 steps/min** is a vigorous-intensity ballpark. These are heuristics, not a lab result for every person. Hills, load, fitness, and height all move the MET cost of the same step rate.

Pillay and colleagues asked people to walk at a self-selected "brisk" pace. They averaged about **118 ± 9 steps/min** — inside the brisk band, close to the 120 heuristic, with no metronome. One hundred is the public-health floor for moderate. A self-chosen brisk walk is often a little quicker.

Faster on purpose, up in the 120s with a long stride, is power walking: [power walking benefits](/blog/power-walking-benefits).

## Walking cadence is not the same as step length

**Chasing a high walking cadence with tiny shuffling steps is not the goal.** Walking cadence counts footfalls. Step length is how far each footfall travels. You can fake 120 steps/min by chopping your stride in half and still be slow in miles per hour and light on METs.

The point of the 100-step rule is intensity: a normal stride at a rate near 3 METs. Short, quick steps from pain, tight shoes, or a crowded sidewalk make the cadence number overstate the workout. Fix the stride first. Then count.

A marathon is the opposite problem: a huge step total at a running rhythm, not a walking target. Scale only: [how many steps in a marathon](/blog/how-many-steps-in-a-marathon). Daily totals are a different question. Ten thousand steps can be slow indoor walking or a day with real brisk bouts. Volume versus a useful dose: [is 10,000 steps a day enough](/blog/is-10000-steps-a-day-enough). Keep the brisk minutes separate from the all-day total. Eight thousand easy steps and a shorter day that includes one real 100-step bout are not the same workout.

## How to count your walking cadence in one minute

You do not need a lab or a footpod.

1. **Walk for at least a minute** so the count is not a standing start.
2. **Count every footfall for 15 seconds and multiply by 4**, or for **10 seconds and multiply by 6**. Twenty-five steps in 15 seconds is 100. Twenty steps in 10 seconds is 120.
3. **Or divide steps in a bout by minutes.** 1,000 steps in 10 minutes is 100 steps/min. 2,400 steps in 20 minutes is 120.

**100 steps/min × 10 minutes = 1,000 steps. × 30 minutes = 3,000 steps.** Ten minutes is the smallest bout worth planning on purpose. What that block is worth: [benefits of walking 10 minutes a day](/blog/benefits-of-walking-10-minutes-a-day). Minutes for a set distance or step count: [walking time calculator](/tools/walking-time-calculator).

Hold **100 steps/min**, or your height-adjusted number, for those 10 minutes. That is **1,000 steps** at moderate intensity if the stride is normal. If it feels easy, try 110–119 and check the talk test before you chase 130. Count on flat ground. A hill raises effort at the same rate. A downhill can hit 110 while you barely work.

## A 10-minute target you can repeat

| Bout | Cadence | Steps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 10 minutes | 100 steps/min | 1,000 |
| 15 minutes | 100 steps/min | 1,500 |
| 30 minutes | 100 steps/min | 3,000 |
| 10 minutes | ~120 steps/min (heuristic, near 4 METs) | 1,200 |

Use one 10-minute bout as the default. Add a second if the week feels easy. Thirty minutes at 100 steps/min is **3,000 brisk steps**, a moderate session, not 3,000 steps shuffled through a workday. Near 152 cm, the same 10 minutes may need ~113 steps/min (about **1,130 steps**) to reach that intensity. Near 198 cm, 10 minutes at ~90 is about **900 steps** and can still be the 3 MET walk. Repeat the bout you can hit again. A one-off 130 you drop the next day does not beat a steady 100.

## Common Questions

### What is a good walking cadence for brisk walking?

About **100 steps per minute** for moderate intensity in many adults. Use ~90 if you are around 198 cm and ~113 if you are around 152 cm. Confirm with the talk test: full sentences yes, singing no.

### Is 100 steps per minute brisk walking?

Yes, as a public-health heuristic. Tudor-Locke's BJSM review and Rowe et al. (2011) place it near 3 METs for many adults. Self-selected "brisk" in the Pillay group landed higher, about **118 ± 9 steps/min**.

### How do I measure cadence without a watch?

Count every footfall for 15 seconds and multiply by 4, or for 10 seconds and multiply by 6. Or divide the steps in a finished walk by the minutes it took.

### Does a higher cadence always mean a harder walk?

No. Tiny shuffling steps can inflate the rate without raising intensity. Match a normal stride to the 100–119 band, then use the talk test. The 120 and 130 figures are heuristics, not a personal lab result.

## The number that makes a walk brisk

Walking cadence is steps per minute, both feet. **100** is the brisk default for many adults, near 3 METs. Taller walkers aim nearer **90**. Shorter walkers aim nearer **113**. Hold it for **10 minutes** and you have about **1,000** brisk steps, with a normal stride and sentences you can still say.
