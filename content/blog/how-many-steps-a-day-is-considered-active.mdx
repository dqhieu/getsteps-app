# How Many Steps a Day Is Considered Active?

**How many steps a day is considered active?** On the classic Tudor-Locke scale that Cleveland Clinic and MedicineNet still reprint, **active starts at 10,000 steps a day**. If "active" means meeting moderate-to-vigorous physical activity guidelines and capturing most of walking's health benefits, newer research — including Tudor-Locke's 2011 review and a 2025 *Lancet Public Health* analysis — puts the line closer to **7,000–8,000 steps**.

Those two answers are not in conflict. One is a classification label. The other is an outcomes threshold. This guide is about the labels themselves — sedentary, low active, somewhat active, active, highly active — plus miles for each tier and how to climb one category at a time.

## The Tudor-Locke Activity Categories

Researchers Catrine Tudor-Locke and David Bassett published this five-tier scale in 2004 and restated it in later reviews. It remains the chart most medical sites use when they classify daily steps.

| Daily step count | Activity category |
|------------------|-------------------|
| Under 5,000 | Sedentary |
| 5,000–7,499 | Low active |
| 7,500–9,999 | Somewhat active |
| 10,000–12,499 | Active |
| 12,500 or more | Highly active |

A later refinement split sedentary into **basal activity** (under 2,500 steps) and **limited activity** (2,500–4,999). If your tracker shows 3,000 steps, you are still sedentary on the main scale — just not at the very bottom.

The [average person walks about 5,000 steps a day](/blog/how-many-steps-does-the-average-person-walk-a-day) worldwide, and closer to 4,800 in the United States. That puts a typical adult on the line between sedentary and low active — not "somewhat active," and nowhere near the classic active cutoff.

## How Many Steps a Day Is Considered Active on the Classic Scale

The classic answer to **how many steps a day is considered active** is **10,000**. That is the floor of Tudor-Locke's "active" band (10,000–12,499). "Highly active" begins at 12,500.

Each label describes volume of movement, not a fitness-test score:

- **Sedentary (under 5,000).** Mostly sitting. Errands and household loops can still land here. Tudor-Locke later treated this band as a sedentary-lifestyle index linked to higher BMI and cardiometabolic risk.
- **Low active (5,000–7,499).** Some walking, not enough to clear public-health guidelines for most people. Many office workers live here on a good weekday.
- **Somewhat active (7,500–9,999).** Regular walking is part of the day — usually a deliberate walk plus incidental movement.
- **Active (10,000–12,499).** The original "active" label. Roughly 4.5–5 miles for a typical adult stride.
- **Highly active (12,500+).** A high-volume day: walking commutes, on-your-feet jobs, or a long walk stacked on an already mobile baseline.

The increments are deliberately round: about **2,500 steps between tiers**. Crossing from 9,900 to 10,100 does not flip a metabolic switch. The labels exist so you can place yourself on a shared scale.

## How Many Steps a Day Is Considered Active in Newer Research

The 2011 Tudor-Locke review in the *International Journal of Behavioral Nutrition and Physical Activity* ([PMC3197470](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3197470/)) asked a different question than the 2004 labels: how many free-living steps translate to the moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) time that public-health guidelines ask for?

Their answer: a **direct estimate of 7,000–8,000 steps a day**, with computed translations spanning about 7,100–11,000 depending on background movement. A cadence of roughly **100 steps per minute** was treated as the floor for moderate intensity. Thirty minutes at that cadence is about 3,000 steps — *on top of* the steps you take just living your day. That is why 7,000–8,000 is the updated "active" number when the question is "am I doing enough moderate activity?" rather than "which Tudor-Locke bucket am I in?"

The 2025 [*Lancet Public Health* dose-response review](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lanpub/article/PIIS2468-2667(25)00164-1/fulltext) (57 studies, more than 160,000 adults) lands in the same neighborhood for outcomes. About **7,000 steps a day** was associated with roughly a 47% lower risk of death from any cause versus ~2,000 steps — nearly matching 10,000. Gains above 7,000 were modest for most outcomes. Whether [7,000 steps a day is enough](/blog/is-7000-steps-a-day-enough) or [8,000 steps a day is good](/blog/is-8000-steps-a-day-good) is an outcomes question; this page is about which label you earn.

So:

| Meaning of "active" | Step threshold |
|---------------------|----------------|
| Classic Tudor-Locke category | **10,000+** |
| Meeting MVPA guidelines (2011 translation) | **~7,000–8,000** |
| Capturing most longevity benefit (2025 evidence) | **~7,000–8,000** |

Use 10,000 when you want a shared classification. Use 7,000–8,000 when you want a realistic activity floor.

## How Many Steps a Day Is Considered Active vs Healthy

"Active" and "healthy" are related, but they are not the same question.

**Healthy** asks what volume is associated with better outcomes — heart disease, mortality, metabolic health. That is the job of our guide on [how many steps a day is healthy](/blog/how-many-steps-a-day-is-healthy). Most adults see the steepest gains by **7,000–10,000**, and you do not need the classic active label to be in a healthy range.

**Active** asks which category you belong to on a step-defined scale. You can be "somewhat active" (7,500–9,999) and still sit in a research-backed healthy zone. You can also hit 10,000 and still have diet, sleep, and strength training left to handle.

Age shifts both numbers. Older adults often capture most longevity benefit closer to 6,000–8,000, so "active enough" at 70 is not the same as at 30. ## How Many Miles Is Each Activity Tier?

Most adults take about **2,000–2,200 steps per mile** walking. Use that range to convert the scale into distance. Your own number depends on height and pace — the [Step Distance Calculator](/tools/step-distance-calculator) will be closer than any chart.

| Activity category | Daily steps | Approx. miles | Approx. km |
|-------------------|-------------|----------------|------------|
| Sedentary | Under 5,000 | Under 2.3–2.5 | Under 3.7–4.0 |
| Low active | 5,000–7,499 | 2.3–3.7 | 3.7–6.0 |
| Somewhat active | 7,500–9,999 | 3.4–5.0 | 5.5–8.0 |
| Active | 10,000–12,499 | 4.5–6.2 | 7.2–10.0 |
| Highly active | 12,500+ | 5.7+ | 9.1+ |

[Walking 2 miles a day](/blog/walking-2-miles-a-day) is roughly 4,000–4,400 steps — still **sedentary** on this scale. The walk is worthwhile; the label just means you have not yet cleared 5,000. Four to five miles is the classic active day. For a distance-first target, see [how many miles should I walk a day](/blog/how-many-miles-should-i-walk-a-day). Calories scale with body weight and pace; a 150 lb adult burns roughly 30–40 calories per 1,000 steps. Check the [Steps to Calories Calculator](/tools/steps-to-calories-calculator) for the energy number next to the label.

## How to Move Up a Step-Activity Tier

Each tier is about **2,500 steps**, which is roughly **1.1–1.3 miles** or **25–30 minutes** of walking at a moderate pace. That is a climb you can plan, not a personality change.

1. **Measure a true baseline.** Track for 7 days without chasing a hero number. Use the weekday average, not your Saturday peak. Most people overestimate until they count.
2. **Pick the next tier, not the top one.** If you average 4,200, the job is 5,000 (low active), not 10,000. Jumping two bands at once is how streaks die by Thursday.
3. **Add 500–1,000 steps per week.** That is a 5–10 minute extra walk. Two to five weeks usually covers one full tier.
4. **Bank a daily "anchor" walk.** A 20–25 minute walk after a meal is about 2,000–2,500 steps — almost one entire tier by itself.
5. **Stack incidental movement.** Walking calls, far parking, one extra transit stop, stairs. These fill the 5,000–7,500 gap without a second workout.
6. **Hold the new tier for two weeks** before chasing the next. Consistency is what the category describes: habitual steps, not a one-off 12,000-step Saturday.

From sedentary to low active, the first 2,000 extra steps matter most. From somewhat active to classic active, you are adding about another mile. Highly active is another mile on top of an already high baseline — useful if you enjoy it, not required for the health curve.

## Common Questions

### How many steps a day is considered active?

**10,000 steps a day** on the classic Tudor-Locke scale (active: 10,000–12,499; highly active: 12,500+). If you mean "active enough to meet MVPA guidelines and capture most health benefits," **7,000–8,000** is the updated answer.

### Is 7,500 steps a day considered active?

**Somewhat active**, not fully "active" on the classic scale. 7,500 is the floor of that middle band. In outcome research, 7,500 is already in the zone where most walking benefits show up.

### Is 8,000 steps a day considered active?

Not on the original five-tier chart — 8,000 sits in **somewhat active**. On the 2011 MVPA translation and 2025 outcome data, 8,000 is a solid "active enough" day for most adults.

### Is 5,000 steps a day considered active?

No. 5,000 is the floor of **low active** and the ceiling of **sedentary**. It is a meaningful step up from 3,000, but it is not an active classification.

### How many steps a day is considered highly active?

**12,500 or more.** That is roughly 5.7–6.3 miles for a typical adult stride.

### Does pace change which category I am in?

The Tudor-Locke categories are based on **total daily steps**, not speed. A brisk cadence (around 100+ steps per minute) does change whether those steps count as moderate-intensity activity. Slow shuffling to 10,000 still earns the classic "active" label; it may not equal 30 minutes of MVPA.

### How many steps a day is considered active for seniors?

The same category labels apply, but the healthy target is often lower. Many adults 60+ capture most longevity benefit around 6,000–8,000 steps, which maps to low active through somewhat active on the classic chart.
