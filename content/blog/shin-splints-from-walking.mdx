# Shin Splints From Walking: Causes, Fixes, Recovery

**Shin splints from walking are usually a load problem, not a walking problem.** The shin gets sore when the lower leg is asked to do more than it is ready for: a sudden jump in daily steps, a faster pace, new hills, hard pavement, or shoes that have worn flat. Most mild cases settle with a short stretch of reduced walking, simple strength work, and a gradual build back up.

Walking feels gentle, but every step lands on a heel and the front shin muscle has to catch the foot as it drops. Do that 10,000 times a day after a month of 4,000 and the shin complains. Below: why walkers get shin pain, walking-specific fixes, four exercises, a week-by-week return plan, and the signs that mean you should see a clinician.

## What Are Shin Splints?

"Shin splints" is the everyday name for pain along the shinbone (tibia) that comes on with activity. The classic version is **medial tibial stress syndrome (MTSS)**: a diffuse ache along the inner edge of the shinbone, usually in the lower half of the leg. It is an overuse injury of the bone and the tissue attached to it.

Walkers often notice a second pattern: soreness on the **front** of the shin, just outside the bone. That is the tibialis anterior, the muscle that lifts your toes and lowers the foot after each heel strike.

| | Inner-edge shin pain (MTSS) | Front-of-shin pain |
|---|---|---|
| Where it hurts | Inner border of the shinbone, lower leg | Front and slightly outside of the shin |
| What it feels like | Dull ache over a few inches, tender to press | Tight, tired, burning muscle |
| Common trigger | Big jump in total walking, hard surfaces, flat feet | Fast pace, long strides, downhill walking |
| Typical pattern | Aches at the start and afterward | Builds during the walk, fades with rest |

Both count as shin splints from walking, and both respond to the same plan: less load for a while, then a gradual return with stronger lower legs.

## Why Walking Causes Shin Splints

### The Front Shin Muscle Brakes Every Step

When your heel touches down, the foot wants to slap the ground. The tibialis anterior lowers it under control. At an easy pace that is small work. At a fast pace with a long stride, it is a lot of braking repeated thousands of times. That is why shin pain when walking often shows up the first time you push into [brisk walking](/blog/what-is-brisk-walking).

### Overstriding

Reaching the foot far in front of the body lands the heel at a steeper angle and lengthens that braking phase. Many walkers lengthen their stride to go faster. Shorter, quicker steps do the same job with less strain on the shin. See [average step length](/blog/average-step-length) for typical step sizes and [how to walk properly](/blog/how-to-walk-properly) for posture and foot strike.

### Doing Too Much, Too Soon

This is the most common cause of shin splints from walking. A new step goal, a sightseeing vacation, a charity walk, or a fresh treadmill habit can double daily steps in a week. Muscles adapt within weeks. Bone remodels more slowly, and the gap between the two is where shin pain appears.

### Surfaces, Hills, Shoes, and Feet

- **Hard or sloped surfaces:** concrete is less forgiving than asphalt or packed trails, and the cambered edge of a road tilts one foot lower than the other.
- **Hills:** downhill walking increases braking work for the front shin. Steep [incline walking](/blog/incline-walking-benefits) loads the calves and shins too.
- **Worn shoes:** cushioning breaks down with mileage. A common rule of thumb is to replace walking shoes every **300 to 500 miles**, sooner if the sole looks compressed or tilted.
- **Foot and body factors:** flat feet or overpronation, tight calves, a previous episode of shin splints, and extra load like a [weighted vest](/blog/walking-with-weighted-vest) all raise the odds.

## How to Treat Shin Splints From Walking

Most cases can be managed at home. The goal is to calm the irritated tissue without losing fitness, then rebuild tolerance.

1. **Relative rest.** Cut walking back to a level that does not increase pain: shorter, slower, flatter walks, or a few days of everyday steps only.
2. **Ice after activity.** 15 to 20 minutes, with a cloth between ice and skin.
3. **Low-impact cross-training.** Cycling, swimming, or pool walking keep fitness while the shin settles. Compare options in [walking vs cycling](/blog/walking-vs-cycling) and [walking vs swimming](/blog/walking-vs-swimming).
4. **Stretch the calves.** With the knee straight and with the knee bent, 30 seconds each, a few times a day.
5. **Strengthen the lower leg.** The exercises below are the part most people skip, and they help keep shin splints from coming back.
6. **Return gradually**, using the week-by-week plan below.

Over-the-counter pain relievers help some people with comfort. Check with a pharmacist or doctor first if you have stomach, kidney, or heart conditions or take other medications.

## Walking-Specific Fixes for Shin Pain

Most shin splints advice is written for runners. These fixes are for walkers.

- **Take shorter, quicker steps.** Land with the foot closer to under your hips. Speed comes from cadence, not reach.
- **Slow down for a week or two** and hold off on fast intervals.
- **Pick softer, flatter routes.** Asphalt paths, tracks, or smooth trails over concrete. Walk the flat middle of a path, not the sloped road edge.
- **Use a treadmill for a predictable surface.** See [treadmill vs walking outside](/blog/treadmill-vs-walking-outside).
- **Take downhills with short, slow steps**, or avoid them while you recover.
- **Split long walks.** Two 20-minute walks are often easier on a sore shin than one 40-minute walk.
- **Track shoe mileage.** The [Step Distance Calculator](/tools/step-distance-calculator) turns step totals into miles so you know when a pair is near 300 to 500.
- **Build daily steps slowly.** About 500 to 1,000 extra steps a day every week or two, the same pace used in [how to get 10,000 steps a day](/blog/how-to-get-10000-steps-a-day).

If the ache sits in the back of the lower leg rather than along the bone, read [calf pain when walking](/blog/calf-pain-when-walking).

## Exercises for Shin Splints From Walking

Start these once the sharp stage has passed and they do not increase pain. Do 2 sets of 10 to 15, working up to 3 sets, most days. Muscle fatigue is fine. Sharp or bone pain is a stop sign.

### Tibialis Raises

Stand with your back against a wall, heels about a foot in front of you. Keeping your legs straight, lift your toes toward your shins, then lower slowly. This trains the exact muscle that brakes each heel strike. Step farther from the wall to make it harder.

### Heel Walks

Lift your toes and walk on your heels for 20 to 30 small steps. This builds endurance in the front shin muscles.

### Toe Walks

Rise onto the balls of your feet and walk 20 to 30 steps. This works the calves and the deep muscles that support the arch, which take load off the inner shin.

### Calf Raises

Rise onto your toes, pause, and lower over about three seconds. Progress to single-leg raises, then raises off the edge of a step.

## A Return-to-Walking Plan by Week

Move to the next week only when the current one feels easy, with no pain during the walk and no extra soreness the next morning. If the shin flares, drop back a week. Mild discomfort that fades quickly is usually acceptable. Pain that builds, changes how you walk, or lingers into the next day is not.

| Week | Walking | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Rest phase (a few days to 1 week) | Everyday steps only | Cross-train, ice, gentle stretching |
| Week 1 | 10 to 15 min, easy pace, flat route, every other day | Start tibialis raises and calf raises |
| Week 2 | 15 to 20 min, easy pace, 4 to 5 days | Add heel walks and toe walks |
| Week 3 | 20 to 30 min, easy to moderate pace, most days | Short stretches of normal pace |
| Week 4 | 30 to 40 min, normal pace | Add gentle hills or a few brisk minutes |
| Week 5 and on | Back toward your usual routine | Add about 500 to 1,000 daily steps every week or two |

From week 3 on, the [walking workout plan for beginners](/blog/walking-workout-plan-beginners) builds pace and duration in small steps. The [Walking Time Calculator](/tools/walking-time-calculator) matches minutes to distance, and the [Daily Step Goal Calculator](/tools/daily-step-goal-calculator) gives you a sensible target once you are back to normal.

Adding jogging later? A structured [walk-run method](/blog/walk-run-method) lets the shins adapt to impact in short doses. Training for something long, like [walking 20 miles](/blog/how-long-does-it-take-to-walk-20-miles), deserves extra weeks of build-up.

## How Long Do Shin Splints Take to Heal?

It depends on how irritated the shin is and how quickly you reduce the load.

- **Mild cases** caught early often settle within **one to three weeks** of modified walking.
- **Moderate cases** commonly take **several weeks**, sometimes two to three months, to return to full activity.
- **Cases that keep getting pushed through** can drag on for months, and ongoing overload can progress to a stress fracture, which has a longer timeline and needs medical guidance.

The earlier you back off, the faster shin splints from walking tend to clear.

## When to See a Doctor

See a doctor, physiotherapist, or sports medicine clinician if you notice:

- **Pain in one small spot on the bone** you can cover with a fingertip, especially if hopping on that leg hurts. This can point to a stress fracture.
- **Pain at rest or at night**, or pain that keeps getting worse despite cutting back.
- **Swelling, redness, or warmth** over the shin.
- **Tightness, burning, numbness, tingling, or foot weakness** that builds during walking. This can be a sign of exertional compartment syndrome.
- **Shin pain after a fall, twist, or direct blow.**
- **No improvement after two to three weeks** of reduced activity.

Get **urgent care** for severe leg pain out of proportion to what happened, a leg that feels very tight and swollen, or a calf that is swollen, red, warm, and painful, which can signal a blood clot.

## Common Questions

### Can you get shin splints from walking?

Yes. Shin splints from walking are common after a sudden increase in daily steps, a switch to faster walking, more hills, harder surfaces, or worn-out shoes. The front shin muscle works on every heel strike, and the shinbone needs time to adapt to extra load.

### Should I keep walking with shin splints?

Usually yes, at a reduced level, as long as it does not make the pain worse during or after the walk. Shorten the distance, slow the pace, and choose flat, softer routes. Sharp or pinpoint bone pain means stop and get it checked.

### Why do my shins hurt when I walk fast?

Fast walking usually means longer strides and a harder heel strike, so the tibialis anterior has to brake the foot more forcefully on every step. Shorter, quicker steps and a gradual build in speed typically ease shin pain when walking fast.

### How do I prevent shin splints when walking?

Increase daily steps slowly, take shorter steps, replace shoes every 300 to 500 miles or so, mix in softer surfaces, and strengthen the lower legs with tibialis raises, heel walks, toe walks, and calf raises.

### How long do shin splints from walking last?

Mild cases often ease in one to three weeks with modified activity. Stubborn cases can take several weeks to a few months. Pain that persists, worsens, or narrows to one spot on the bone needs a medical assessment.

## Walk Your Way Back

Shin splints from walking mean the load went up faster than your lower legs could adapt. Back off for a short while, take shorter steps, choose kinder surfaces and fresh shoes, and strengthen the front shin and calves. Then build up a week at a time.
