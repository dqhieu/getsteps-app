# Average Hiking Speed: Pace by Terrain, Grade & Pack Weight

The average hiking speed is **2 miles per hour**, with most hikers falling somewhere between 1.5 and 3.5 mph. That is noticeably slower than the average walking pace on pavement, and the gap is not about fitness. Trails are uneven, they climb, and you are usually carrying weight.

Roughly 62% of hikers report moving at about 2 mph on normal rolling terrain, while about 32% report closer to 3 mph. If you are planning a route and need one number to start from, use 2 mph and adjust from there.

## Average Hiking Speed by Terrain

Terrain is the single biggest factor in your trail pace. The same person can vary by 2 mph across a single day depending on what is underfoot.

| Terrain | Typical Speed | Miles per Hour of Effort |
|---------|--------------|--------------------------|
| Flat, groomed trail | 3.0–3.5 mph | 3+ miles |
| Rolling singletrack | 2.0–2.5 mph | ~2 miles |
| Rocky or root-covered trail | 1.5–2.0 mph | ~1.75 miles |
| Steep ascent | 1.0–1.5 mph | ~1 mile |
| Scree, sand, or deep snow | 0.5–1.0 mph | under 1 mile |

On flat terrain with a light pack, most people can cover at least 3 miles in an hour. On a sustained steep climb, 1 mph is realistic even for strong hikers.

## How Elevation Gain Slows You Down

Distance alone is a poor predictor of hiking time. A 6-mile trail with 3,000 feet of gain takes far longer than a 6-mile trail on the flat, and the difference is not small.

A practical rule used by hikers and search-and-rescue planners alike: add **one hour for every 2,000 feet of elevation gain**, on top of your flat-ground time estimate.

| Route | Distance | Gain | Flat-Time Estimate | Realistic Estimate |
|-------|----------|------|--------------------|--------------------|
| Valley loop | 6 mi | 300 ft | 3.0 hr | ~3.1 hr |
| Ridge climb | 6 mi | 2,000 ft | 3.0 hr | ~4.0 hr |
| Summit push | 8 mi | 4,000 ft | 4.0 hr | ~6.0 hr |

Descending is faster than climbing but not as fast as flat ground. Steep downhill sections punish the knees and force most hikers to slow to 2 to 2.5 mph.

## How Pack Weight Changes Your Pace

Pack weight has a compounding effect. It slows you directly, and it tires you faster, which slows you further over the back half of a long day.

| Pack Weight | Typical Speed Penalty |
|-------------|----------------------|
| Daypack (10 lb or less) | negligible |
| Overnight (20–25 lb) | 0.3–0.5 mph slower |
| Multi-day (35–45 lb) | 0.5–1.0 mph slower |

A common guideline is to keep a loaded pack under 20% of your body weight. Past that, pace and enjoyment both fall off sharply.

## Hiking Speed vs Walking Speed

Most people are surprised by how much slower hiking is than their everyday walk. The [average walking pace](/blog/average-walking-pace) on flat pavement is about 3 to 3.5 mph, while trail hiking averages 2 mph.

| Activity | Typical Speed |
|----------|--------------|
| Casual walk (pavement) | 2.5–3.0 mph |
| Brisk walk (pavement) | 3.5–4.0 mph |
| Trail hiking | 1.5–3.0 mph |
| Speed hiking | 4.0–5.0 mph |
| Trail running | 6.0–10.0 mph |

## Naismith's Rule: The Classic Estimate

Naismith's Rule has been used to plan hikes since 1892 and still works as a rough baseline:

**Allow 1 hour for every 3 miles of distance, plus 1 hour for every 2,000 feet of ascent.**

For a 9-mile hike with 2,000 feet of gain:
- 9 miles ÷ 3 = 3 hours
- 2,000 ft ÷ 2,000 = 1 hour
- **Estimated time: 4 hours**

The rule assumes a fit hiker on reasonable terrain with a light load. Add 25% to 50% for a heavy pack, difficult footing, a large group, or poor weather. It also does not include breaks, and breaks add up quickly on a full-day route.

## Factors That Affect Your Hiking Speed

**Fitness and trail experience**
Cardiovascular fitness sets your ceiling on climbs. Experience sets your speed on technical ground, because experienced hikers read the trail ahead and place their feet with less hesitation.

**Altitude**
Above roughly 8,000 feet, thinner air reduces the oxygen available to your muscles. Expect to lose 10% to 20% of your normal pace, more if you have not acclimatized.

**Group size**
Groups move at the pace of their slowest member, and larger groups stop more often. A party of six will almost always be slower than a party of two.

**Weather and trail conditions**
Mud, wet rock, ice, and heat all cut pace. Hot weather in particular forces more frequent water and rest stops.

**Time on feet**
Nearly everyone slows in the final third of a long hike. Plan your turnaround time using your expected *late-day* pace, not your fresh morning pace.

## How to Estimate Your Own Hiking Speed

Averages are a starting point. Your own number is more useful, and it takes one hike to find it.

1. Pick a trail you know with a documented distance and elevation gain.
2. Track the hike with a step and distance tracker, noting total moving time separately from break time.
3. Divide distance by moving time to get your true pace.
4. Repeat on a steeper trail and a flatter trail to learn your personal range.

Once you know you hike, say, 2.4 mph on rolling terrain and 1.3 mph on sustained climbs, route planning becomes far more accurate than any published average.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a good hiking speed?

For most hikers on rolling terrain, 2 to 3 mph is a good pace. On steep or technical trails, 1 to 1.5 mph is perfectly normal and not a sign of poor fitness. Pace matters less than finishing comfortably with daylight to spare.

### How long does it take to hike 10 miles?

At the 2 mph average, 10 miles takes about 5 hours of moving time. Add elevation gain at roughly 1 hour per 2,000 feet, plus breaks. A 10-mile hike with 2,500 feet of gain realistically takes 6.5 to 7 hours door to door.

### How many steps is a 5-mile hike?

A 5-mile hike is roughly 11,000 to 12,500 steps for most adults. Trail hiking tends to produce slightly more steps per mile than pavement walking because uneven ground shortens your stride. See [how many steps are in a mile](/blog/how-many-steps-in-a-mile) for stride-based figures.

### Is hiking 3 mph fast?

Yes. Sustaining 3 mph on trail puts you in roughly the top third of hikers. On flat, groomed trail with a light daypack it is achievable for many fit walkers, but holding 3 mph across mixed terrain with elevation is genuinely quick.

### Does hiking burn more calories than walking?

Considerably more. Uneven terrain, elevation gain, and pack weight all raise the metabolic cost. Hiking typically burns 1.5 to 2 times the calories of flat walking at the same speed. Our [calories burned hiking](/blog/calories-burned-hiking) guide breaks this down by weight and grade.

### How do I hike faster?

Build aerobic fitness with regular walking or stair work, lighten your pack, use trekking poles on climbs and descents, and shorten your breaks rather than your stride. Consistent moving time beats short bursts of speed.
